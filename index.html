The Income-tax Act, 2025 and the Income-tax Act, 1961: a structural and financial comparison

This assignment compares the Income-tax Act, 1961 — as most recently amended by the Finance Act, 2025 — with the Income-tax Act, 2025, which repeals and replaces it with effect from 1 April 2026 IT Dept FAQ. It identifies what is genuinely new, what is merely renumbered, and what continues unchanged, before quantifying the financial effect on an individual taxpayer with worked examples.

Central argument

"The transition is best understood as two independent developments that share a headline: a structural recodification that changes how the law reads, and a Finance Act 2025 rate change that changes what a taxpayer owes. The two are often conflated in popular commentary; this assignment treats them separately throughout."

01 — Analytical framework

Similarities and differences at a glance

Before the detailed comparison, this section separates what carries over from what has genuinely changed — the organising distinction for the rest of the assignment.

What both Acts share
6 points

  • Both regimes (old and new) remain available to the taxpayer
  • Same basic filing cycle, PAN framework, and refund mechanics
  • Chapter VI-A style deductions (80C, 80D family) preserved, only renumbered
  • No retrospective effect — pending 1961 Act matters continue under it §536 savings clause
D
What genuinely differs
6 points
  • 819 sections condensed to 536, with materially fewer provisos IT Dept FAQ
  • Dual Previous Year / Assessment Year system replaced by a single Tax Year
  • Forty-plus scattered TDS sections consolidated into essentially one (§393)
  • New disclosure and penalty intensity for foreign assets and NRIs
  • Broadened statutory definition of Virtual Digital Assets
  • Presumptive scheme compliance relief for small business under §63

03 — Structural analysis

Architecture, in detail

The 1961 Act accumulated roughly 4,000 amendments across 65 Finance Acts over six decades Taxguru, producing genuine navigational difficulty — provisions like the old Section 10A remained on the books for over a decade after losing practical relevance KDK Software. The 2025 Act's stated objective is re-presentation of the same tax policy, not its alteration IT Dept FAQ.

Income-tax Act, 1961 Repealed 01.04.2026
  • 819 sections, ~4,000 amendments across 65 Finance Acts
  • Dual time concept — Previous Year and Assessment Year
  • TDS scattered across 40+ sections (192, 194A–194S...)
  • Long-form prose, nested provisos and explanations
  • Separate Forms 3CA, 3CB, 3CD for audit reporting
  • Governs all income earned up to 31 March 2026
Income-tax Act, 2025 In force 01.04.2026
  • 536 sections, 23 chapters, 16 schedules
  • Single unified concept — Tax Year
  • TDS consolidated into essentially one section (393)
  • Tables and formulas replace scattered prose
  • Unified Form 26 replaces 3CA / 3CB / 3CD
  • Governs all income earned from 1 April 2026 onward

Why the Tax Year concept matters analytically

Under the 1961 Act, income earned in a Previous Year (say 2025-26) is assessed in the following Assessment Year (2026-27) — a dual-year reference that the Income Tax Department itself identifies as a recurring source of taxpayer confusion IT Dept FAQ. Section 536(3) of the 2025 Act provides that references to a Tax Year for pre-2026 periods are legally read as the corresponding Previous Year under the old Act, which is the mechanism that prevents any gap or overlap in coverage between the two statutes.

12 — Conclusion

Conclusion

Two stories, one transition

The transition from the Income-tax Act, 1961 to the Income-tax Act, 2025 is best understood as two separate developments that happen to share a single headline. The first is a genuine structural achievement: a 65-year-old statute, burdened by roughly 4,000 amendments and a confusing dual-year system, has been reorganised into a leaner, table-driven code of 536 sections IT Dept FAQ — a change of real value to compliance, litigation, and administration, even though it alters no one's tax bill by itself.

The second is a genuine financial reform that predates and is independent of the Income-tax Act, 2025: the Finance Act, 2025's enhancement of the Section 87A rebate and standard deduction, which raised the effective tax-free income ceiling for salaried individuals from approximately ₹7.75 lakh to ₹12.75 lakh Tax2win. This is the change that actually moves money in or out of a taxpayer's pocket, and it would have had an identical effect even had the 1961 Act never been recodified.

A well-constructed comparative answer therefore separates the architecture from the arithmetic — crediting the Income-tax Act, 2025 for making the law easier to read and administer, while correctly attributing the rupee savings demonstrated in Illustrations 1 and 2 to the Finance Act, 2025 rate and rebate changes that both the old Act (for FY 2025-26) and the new Act (for FY 2026-27 onward) now carry forward identically.
